    Why Choose HRL Plumbing for Grease Trap Cleaning in Dublin, OH?

    A grease trap works by slowing the water down long enough for fat to float and food to sink. Past about 25 percent full, the separation stops working. The grease then slips past the outlet into the lateral, cools into a hard white crust, and a cable machine only bores a narrow hole through it.

    • Complete Pump-Outs, We pull grease, water and the solids on the floor every time.
    • System Longevity, Less grease in the line means fewer line jettings and a longer life for the drain pipe.
    • Prevent Backups, Pumping on schedule keeps grease from slipping past the outlet and setting up in the line.

    Citywide Coverage

    School cafeterias and church kitchens around Dublin usually have small traps that get forgotten for a year at a time. Those go on a schedule as well. A once-a-quarter pump-out is often enough for a kitchen that runs lunch five days a week and sits idle in summer.

    Frequently Asked Questions About Grease Trap Cleaning in Dublin

    Small under-sink traps often start around $200 a visit. A large interceptor with a hardened grease cap can run past $1,000. Disposal is billed by the gallon at the receiving facility, so the gallons hauled drive most of the cost, and a 1,500-gallon tank fills a truck.

    Fryer volume drives the schedule more than seat count. Monthly pumping is common for busy restaurants. Lighter kitchens, like a coffee shop with a small food menu, can stretch to every two or three months as long as depth readings stay under the 25 percent line.

    Under-sink hydromechanical traps and in-ground gravity interceptors make up most of our work. Automatic grease removal units are on the list too. Those AGRUs have a heater and a skimmer wheel that pull grease into a container, and they need the container emptied and the wheel and screen cleaned on a schedule.

    Rules come from your sewer district or city pretreatment program. Most follow some version of the 25 percent rule. Plenty also cap the interval, want a year or more of records on site, and ban enzymes in place of pumping, so we look up your program before we schedule.

    It is the most common pumping standard. When grease and solids reach a quarter of the depth, it is time to pump. Past that point the water moves through too fast for grease to separate, so grease rides out the outlet even though the trap looks like it is working.

    The terms get mixed up constantly. Generally a trap is the small unit indoors, often under 100 gallons. An interceptor is the big in-ground tank outside, often 500 gallons or more, and plumbing codes treat them as separate categories with different sizing rules.

    Skimming off the top layer yourself is usually allowed. The disposal is the part that gets people fined. Many programs want a licensed hauler's record for every cleaning, and a kitchen doing its own has nothing to show the inspector.

    It depends on trap size and menu. Most restaurants end up on a two-week to three-month interval. Homes with a small unit may go a year, but a fryer-heavy kitchen on a 50-gallon trap might need pumping every other week.
