    Slab Leak Warning Signs Every Ohio Homeowner Should Know

    By Aaron Mitchell · October 15, 2026 · 5 min read

    Pour a slab, lay soft copper or PEX in the gravel underneath, and you get a house that is cheap to build and solid for decades. Most houses built in the last half century went up on that kind of slab, with the supply lines running through the base gravel. The catch is simple. When one of those lines opens up, nothing shows on the surface, and the water soaks into the base gravel for weeks before anybody notices. Catching it early keeps a pinhole from becoming a flooring and mold job.

    Why Slab Leaks Happen in Ohio Homes

    Under-slab copper fails for mechanical reasons first. Builders from the 1970s into the early 2000s often laid soft copper straight in the trench before the pour. Over thirty or forty years, that tubing rubs against gravel and rebar every time it moves, and the wall wears thin at the contact point. Soil adds its own stress.

    Tree roots and shifting clay often crack older clay or cast iron sewer laterals, leading to slow drains and backups.

    Expansive clay swells when it gets wet and shrinks back in a dry spell. Every swell and shrink cycle shoves the pipe against whatever rock sits next to it. Buried stones and hard pan act as pressure points, and a copper line pinned against one of them takes a little more abuse each season until the wall gives out at that spot. Nobody sees it happen.

    Water chemistry does its share of the damage. Hard water and the chlorine or chloramine that utilities add for disinfection can pit copper from the inside, and those pits deepen until one breaks through the wall. Hot lines usually fail first. Each hot draw makes the line grow and shrink a hair, and that motion slowly saws the copper where it touches the slab.

    Private wells serve many rural homes, and iron, sulfur and hardness are common issues that call for filtration or softening.

    The Warning Signs to Watch For

    Slab leaks almost never show up as puddles. Instead you get a string of small clues, each easy to explain away by itself, that point to water going somewhere you cannot see. These are the questions we ask on the first call:

    • A warm spot on the floor. A leaking hot line heats the concrete above it, and bare feet on tile tend to notice it before anything else.
    • The meter moving with everything off. Shut off every fixture, the irrigation and the ice maker, then watch the small triangle or star dial on the meter for 15 minutes.
    • A bill that jumps for no reason. Compare it with the same month last year, since irrigation season skews month to month comparisons.
    • Running water you can hear under the floor or behind a wall when every tap is closed.
    • A water heater that never seems to rest, because it keeps refilling with cold water to replace what is leaking out.
    • Damp carpet pad or cupped hardwood with no leak you can point to.
    • A musty smell along the baseboards, especially in interior rooms with no plumbing nearby.
    • New cracks in tile or drywall that show up alongside any of the signs above.

    One clue by itself proves nothing. A moving meter plus a warm spot, though, is plenty of reason to book leak detection. We see the meter test catch leaks that people had been living with for months.

    Finding the Leak Without Tearing Up the House

    Leak detection is a lot less destructive than most people picture. The first hour goes to splitting the system into hot and cold and putting a gauge on each side to find the line that will not hold. Then the acoustic gear comes out, a ground microphone that picks up the hiss of water forcing its way out of a pinhole. On the hot side, a thermal imaging camera shows the heat trail of the leaking line right through the tile. Most of the time we can mark the spot within a foot or two before any concrete gets cut.

    Then you pick a fix. A spot repair means cutting a small opening in the slab, replacing the bad section and patching the concrete. A reroute abandons the leaking line under the slab and runs new PEX through the attic or walls instead. A full repipe makes sense after the second or third slab leak. Spot repairs often run about $1,500 to $4,000 depending on access and flooring, and a single-line reroute commonly lands between $1,000 and $3,500.

    What to Do in the Meantime

    Find your main shutoff today, before you need it in a hurry. In a lot of houses it sits near the front hose bib or in a curb box out by the street, under a lid you may need a screwdriver to pop. If the meter test confirms a leak and water starts surfacing, close the main. If the hot side is the problem, put the water heater on vacation or pilot so the burner or elements do not run with the supply shut off. Take photos of any damaged flooring. Many homeowners policies pay to access and repair the leak even when they exclude the pipe itself.

    When to Call a Professional

    This is not a weekend project. Cutting concrete without knowing exactly where the leak is wastes a day, and newer slabs often carry post-tension cables, and nicking one is a structural problem, not a plumbing one.
